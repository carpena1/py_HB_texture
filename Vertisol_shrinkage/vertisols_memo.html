Converting vertisol retention curves with a shrinking bulk density: two examples

Laikipia (Kenya) and Tomejil (Andalusia, Spain) · py_HB_texture · R. Muñoz-Carpena, 31 October 2026

Why

Retention curves are often measured as gravimetric water and made volumetric with one bulk density per sample. In a vertisol that misrepresents the soil: swelling clays shrink as they dry, so their bulk density rises along the curve, and one wet-state density understates the water held at the dry end. This note applies the same variable-density correction to two vertisols from two regions, shows it step by step for one sample of each, compares how much it changes the curves, and reports its effect on the texture and Ks the tool predicts from them.

Steps, at each measured suction

  1. Gravimetric water: w = θ / BD₁, with BD₁ the one density above. Both soils' curves are already gravimetric as published, so w is taken directly; the standard mass–volume relation, with water density 1 g/cm³ (Hillel 1998), links it to the one-density curve θ₁ = w · BD₁.
  2. Specific volume under normal shrinkage, where the soil loses volume equal to the water it loses: v = 1/ρs + w, with ρs = 2.65 g/cm³, the conventional mineral particle density (Blake and Hartge 1986) (cm³ of soil per g of solids). This is the saturation line of the shrinkage curve, the "normal" phase of Haines (1923) and Stirk (1954), in the specific-volume form of McGarry and Malafant (1987); Bronswijk (1988) used it to model swelling clays.
  3. Bulk density: BD = min(cap, 1/v). The cap stands for the density at which shrinkage stops. For Laikipia it is 1.6 g/cm³ (shrinkage limit w = 1/1.6 − 1/2.65 = 0.248), the value of the published volumetric series (Willard et al. 2026), which is also the dry-end density reported for the Tomejil vertisol (Vanderlinden et al. 2021). For Tomejil it is 1.75 g/cm³ (w = 0.194), the median oven-dry clod density of US Vertisols like these two (KSSL; clay 45–65 %, clod density at 33 kPa below 1.25 g/cm³; 1.54–1.90 for the middle 80 %; Soil Survey Staff 2014a and the KSSL database). Both caps give the same texture results (see How general is the cap? below). Where the soil is wetter than the core was dense, 1/v is below BD₁: the core had swollen, and the conversion lowers θ. This step is a simplification: measured shrinkage curves have structural, normal, residual and zero phases (Mitchell 1992; Groenevelt and Grant 2001; Peng and Horn 2005; Cornelis et al. 2006), and in the residual phase air enters and shrinkage slows. Here normal shrinkage runs up to the cap and then stops, with no residual phase, and the cap is not measured on each soil.
  4. New volumetric water: θ_new = w · BD, the mass–volume relation of step 1 with the shrinking density. The one-density curve is θ₁ = w · BD₁.

How general is the cap?

The two caps used here, 1.6 g/cm³ for Laikipia (as published) and 1.75 g/cm³ for Tomejil, can be checked against a general source, the US National Cooperative Soil Survey Soil Characterization Database (KSSL): every natural clod is weighed and measured at 33 kPa and again oven-dry (Saran-coated clod method; Grossman et al. 1968; Soil Survey Staff 2014a), the measurement behind the coefficient of linear extensibility (COLE) used to recognize Vertisols (Soil Survey Staff 2014b). In the model of step 3, the oven-dry clod density is the cap: the density a clod reaches once it has stopped shrinking. Drawn from the database's horizons with both densities:

Across them the oven-dry density rises with the 33 kPa density (oven-dry ≈ 0.615 × BD₃₃ + 1.011, r = 0.50), which gives a per-sample cap: 1.67 for WL_3LT07414 (BD₁ 1.07), 1.70 and 1.68 for the Tomejil treatments.

1.6 and 1.75 bracket the likely value. The oven-dry density is an upper bound for where normal shrinkage ends, since real clods keep shrinking slowly through the residual phase before they are fully dry (the long normal phase of Texas Vertisols in Yule and Ritchie 1980 is one measured example); the model of step 3 has no residual phase, so a 1.75 cap would push the dry end slightly too high, and 1.6 errs the other way. The texture result does not depend on which is used (each soil predicted as a new source, its own table removed from the reference; test_caps.py):

Raising the cap from 1.6 to 1.75 changes only the dry end, where the curves are already flat: single samples' predicted clay moves by up to 8.4 points (Laikipia) and 3.9 (Tomejil), the means by 1.2 points or less, one Laikipia sample changes class and every Tomejil ring keeps its class. A cap of 1.4, below the shrinkage limit of most vertisols, loses most of the gain for Laikipia (57 % exact) and part of it for Tomejil.

What the two examples show

  • The correction behaves alike through the pressure-plate range. θ rises by about a quarter at pF 2, by a third or more at 33 kPa, and by about half at 1500 kPa (50 % for Laikipia, 58 % for Tomejil, median).
  • They differ at the wet end. Near saturation the Laikipia cores had swollen beyond their ring density (θ₁ above the porosity of BD₁ in 42 of the 70 samples), so the shrinking density there is below BD₁ and θ falls slightly (median ratio 0.99, 0.90 for WL_3LT07414). Tomejil's rings sit on the shrinkage line, and θ rises by 5 %.
  • Both reach their cap at the dry end. Every Laikipia sample with a 1500 kPa point reaches 1.6 (WL_3LT07414: w = 0.234 at 1500 kPa, below the limit of 0.248), and 36 of the 54 Tomejil rings reach 1.75.
  • Texture improves in both. With one density both soils read too coarse and seemed to lose water steeply just below saturation (50 % and 65 % of their saturated water left at 330 cm, against 82 % for the reference clays). With the shrinking density they hold 73 % and 81 %, and every clay in both soils is read as clay. The predicted clay matches the measured mean for Tomejil (53 % against 56 %) but stays below it for Laikipia (46 % against 54 %). The result hardly depends on whether the cap is 1.6 or 1.75 (previous section).
  • Ks falls in both, and is low confidence either way. Under normal shrinkage the water lost near saturation is volume lost, not pores draining, so the curve shows few large draining pores: the kNN Ks drops about 6-fold for Laikipia and 25-fold for Tomejil, and the physical estimate about 4-fold. The two estimates then agree within a factor of 10 for 79 % of the Laikipia samples and 17 % of the Tomejil rings (97 % and 93 % with one density). Against the SATURO infiltrometer on 26 Laikipia samples (median 14.0 cm/h), the kNN falls from 92 % to 54 % of samples within a factor of 10, while the physical estimate stays at 92 % (96 % with one density). The low kNN values may describe the swollen matrix, while field values carry cracks and macropores; neither conversion gives a true pore-size distribution for a swelling clay.
  • The agreement is partly built in. Both use the same formula, and their one-density values are similar wet-state densities. The independent support is the texture result, reached separately in each soil.
Caveat. Under normal shrinkage the converted water content equals the porosity of the shrinking soil at every step, so the model treats the sample as still saturated at 1500 kPa. Real vertisols let air in once structural pores and cracks open, so this conversion overstates the dry end, while one fixed density understates it by ignoring shrinkage. The truth lies in between. Measured shrinkage data would pin it down: ring or clod volumes at a few water contents, a shrinkage curve (e.g. Boivin et al. 2004), or a COLE value (Grossman et al. 1968), for either soil.
